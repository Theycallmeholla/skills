# Interview Question Bank

Five phases, in priority order of what makes a case study work. You will rarely need all of them — mine files and conversation first, then only ask what's still missing. Phases 2 and 4 (the before-state and the results) are the heart of a case study; if the user's patience is limited, spend it there.

Every question below is a *pattern*, not a script. Adapt wording to the client and industry, and always pre-fill options with your best guesses from what you already know.

## Phase 1 — Quick facts (30 seconds of clicking)

Goal: enough to anchor everything else. Often fully answerable from files.

- Which client is this? (options: names you've seen in their files/tools)
- What kind of business are they? (guess from the name/website: "Residential plumbing", "CrossFit gym", ...)
- What did you do for them? (options like: "Full website rebuild", "New site + SEO", "Branding + site", "Ongoing marketing")
- Roughly when? ("This year", "Last year", "2+ years ago")

## Phase 2 — The before (the part prospects recognize themselves in)

Goal: the situation a similar prospect is living in right now. Concrete beats dramatic.

- What was the main problem with what they had? ("Outdated/embarrassing site", "No site at all", "Site existed but generated zero leads", "Couldn't be found on Google")
- What made them finally reach out — was there a trigger? ("Lost a big job to a competitor", "Referral told them to call", "Rebrand/new location", "Not sure / gradual")
- What was it costing them? ("Missed calls/leads", "Looked less professional than competitors", "Wasted ad spend", "Hard to say")

## Phase 3 — The work

Goal: just enough to be credible and interesting — this is the phase to keep shortest. Prospects skim it.

- What were the main pieces of the work? (multi-select from what you know: design, copy, SEO, speed, booking system, ads...)
- Was there anything unusual or clever about this one — something you're proud of? ("Yes, let me tell you" free-text is the real answer here; offer "Nothing special, solid execution" as a legitimate option)
- How long from kickoff to launch? ("Under a month", "1-3 months", "Longer", "Don't remember")

## Phase 4 — Results (the credibility engine)

Goal: at least one number and one human reaction. Push gently but take what exists.

- What changed after launch that you can point to? ("More calls/leads", "More traffic", "Better close rate — leads came in warmer", "Client just loves it", "Honestly haven't tracked")
- For any metric they claim: how solid is that number? ("From analytics/reports — I can get the exact figure", "Client told me", "My rough sense") — this determines how the draft phrases it.
- Did the client ever say anything memorable about the work — in a text, email, review, call? ("Yes, I can find it", "Yes, but I'd be paraphrasing", "No — help me ask them for a quote")
- If tracking exists but numbers aren't handy: offer to check connected analytics yourself, or drop a `[NEED: exact figure]` placeholder and move on. Never let a missing number stall the draft.

## Phase 5 — Publishing & media

Goal: what's needed to actually put it on the site.

- Can we use the client's real name and logo? ("Yes, they'd love it", "Probably — I should ask", "No, keep it anonymous")
- What visuals exist? (multi-select: "Before screenshots", "I can screenshot the new site", "Photos of their business/team", "Video or testimonial clip", "Basically nothing")
- What should the reader do at the end? ("Book a call", "Get a free site audit", "See more work", options matching CTAs the user's site already uses)

## Fatigue signals

Short answers getting shorter, "idk", "whatever you think", long pauses, or any hint of annoyance → stop asking, start writing. Announce it: "Good enough — I'm drafting now. Anything I don't have becomes a placeholder you can fill later." This is a feature of the skill, not a failure of the interview.
